What to write in a keepsake book when you have no idea what to write

The blank page is the enemy. Forty real prompts you can answer in under a minute — tonight, in whatever notebook or notes app you already have.

Every keepsake book dies the same death. Pages one through six: birth weight, first foods, a hospital bracelet taped in. Page seven: blank. Page two hundred: blank. Not because you stopped caring — because “write something meaningful” is a terrible assignment, and that’s the assignment a blank page gives you.

Questions fix this, which is why a list of keepsake book prompt ideas beats every volume of advice about journaling more. You cannot write “a meaningful memory” on demand, but you can answer “what do they call you?” in fifteen seconds, and the answer turns out to be the meaningful memory. Here are forty that work. Use them anywhere — a notebook, your notes app, the back of a receipt. The tool doesn’t matter. The question does.

Keepsake book prompt ideas that actually get answered

Two rules make the list below work. First, every question is about this month, not about childhood in general — “what are they obsessed with right now” can be answered instantly and differently every season, while “what were their favorite things” produces the blank page again. Second, none of them require an occasion. Milestones write themselves down; it’s the ordinary Tuesday that needs a question waiting for it.

One more thing before the list: these aren’t newborn questions, and that’s deliberate. Keepsake books are marketed at the first six months and abandoned by the eighteenth, but the questions below work from one to twelve. For a toddler, you answer on their behalf, from observation. Somewhere around six, the better move is to ask them directly and write the answer down word for word — the phrasing is the artifact, so resist the urge to tidy it. If your kid is seven and the book is empty, you are not late. You’re starting with better material.

What they say

  • What did they say this week that you want to remember exactly?
  • What do they call you — every version?
  • How do they say goodbye?
  • What’s their word for the thing they love most?
  • What question do they ask constantly right now?
  • What did they whisper when they thought nobody heard?
  • What do they say when something goes wrong?
  • What’s the current catchphrase, and where did it come from?
  • What did they ask this week that you couldn’t answer?

The mispronunciations age the best, which is why the one in the card leads the set. Kids fix their own words without warning — one Tuesday it’s “pasghetti” and by Friday it isn’t, and nobody announces the change. The written-down version is usually the only one that survives.

What they do

  • What are they obsessed with right now?
  • What are they pretending to be this week?
  • What will they eat only if it’s cut a specific way?
  • What’s the current bedtime negotiation tactic?
  • What do they do with their hands when they concentrate?
  • What made them laugh so hard they fell over?
  • What are they afraid of — right now, this month?
  • How do they wake up — slow, loud, somewhere in between?
  • What game did they invent, and what are its rules as far as you can tell?
  • What do they do the moment they think you’re not watching?

Who they are, this exact month

Kids replace themselves every season and the old version leaves no forwarding address. These are the questions that catch a version before it’s overwritten:

  • What’s changed about them in the last three months?
  • What did they teach themself this month?
  • What’s something they can suddenly do that they couldn’t do at their last birthday?
  • Who is their best friend and what’s the state of that alliance?
  • What opinion do they hold most strongly right now?
  • What do they want to be when they grow up — this week’s answer?
  • What’s the bravest thing they did recently?
  • What’s their favorite place, and what do they do there?
  • What do they refuse, on principle?
  • Which grown-up are they imitating, and what did they pick up?
Two sentences is plenty. The entry you actually write beats the essay you meant to write.

The ones you’ll be gladdest you kept

  • Describe their laugh — actually describe it, the mechanics of it.
  • Write down the story of the day they were born while you still remember the details.
  • Write them a letter about exactly who they are right now; they won’t be this person next year.
  • Write what you’d want them to know if you weren’t around to say it.
  • What do you hope they never grow out of?
  • What about being their parent surprised you most this year?
  • Record their voice telling you a story — any story. The voice guide is its own essay.
  • Measure their hand. Trace it, if you’re on paper.
  • What does an ordinary Tuesday look like right now, hour by hour?
  • What’s the smell you’ll associate with this year?

That last set breaks the two-sentence rule on purpose. Nine of the forty are slower — five-minute entries, not fifteen-second ones — and they’re the ones that read like a letter in twenty years. One a month is plenty.

How to actually keep this up

Don’t schedule it. Scheduled journaling produces guilt, and guilt produces abandoned keepsake books — that’s the death from paragraph one wearing a calendar. A better system: put the list where the blank page currently is. Copy these forty into the front of the notebook, or star this page, and when you have a minute and a memory, take the top question that fits. Answered out of order, half-skipped, in pencil — all fine. There is no behind.

Date every entry, and write the age next to the date. “March 2026, age 3½” costs five seconds now and saves the person reading it — possibly the kid, grown — from doing subtraction against a birthday to work out whether this was the year of the dinosaurs or the year of the volcanoes. And hold yourself to the two-sentence bar in both directions: two sentences is enough to count, and two sentences is allowed to be all of it. “Asked what rain is for. Rejected my answer” is a complete entry. It’s a better entry than most paragraphs, because it got written.

Forty questions is a year of keepsake book if you answer one a week, and a year of keepsake book is a thing almost nobody’s kid has.
