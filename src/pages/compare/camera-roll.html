The real comparison

You already have a camera roll. Here's what it doesn't do.

Most advice on how to organize photos of your kids starts from the idea that the camera roll is the problem. It isn't. It's free, it's already in your pocket, and it's genuinely good at its job. What follows is everything it gets right, the four things it can't do, and an honest answer about whether that matters for you.

The case for it

Everything the camera roll gets right

A comparison that pretends any of this away is an ad. So, plainly:

  • ✓It's free. It came with the phone. There is no plan to pick and no bill to notice.
  • ✓It's already there. No new app, no new habit. It opens from the lock screen, and you've used it every day for years.
  • ✓It's effectively unlimited. You can take ten thousand photos and it will keep accepting them without comment.
  • ✓The search is genuinely great. It finds faces, places, and things you never labeled, and it gets quietly better every year.
  • ✓The backup story is the best in consumer software. Lose the phone in a lake and the pictures are on the next one before you've finished setting it up.

All of that is true. None of it is the problem.

Organizing photos of your kids isn't the problem

The camera roll's job is keeping the pictures, and it does that about as well as it can be done. The gaps are in what never gets into the roll at all, and in what the pictures can't carry on their own. There are four, specifically.

It never asks

The camera comes out when something already looks like a moment — a birthday, a beach, a good haircut. It never comes out for the question you didn't think to ask. What were they afraid of at four? What did they think your job was? A camera roll only holds what you thought of yourself, and the questions turn out to be the hard part. It keeps the picture, not the reason

A photo of a shoe stays a photo of a shoe. You know today that it's the shoe they insisted went on the wrong foot, every day, for a month. The camera roll stores the pixels and none of that. In twenty years the picture will still be there, sharp as ever, and it will mean almost nothing — because the reason was never written down anywhere. The photo survives. The context was never stored at all.

It can't hold what the camera can't see

The ten minutes on the floor being a horse. The thing they said in the bath that made you leave the room so they wouldn't see you laugh. How they pronounced "spaghetti" the year they were three. None of that produces a photograph, so none of it is in the roll. Half of a childhood is spoken, or audible, or just done — and a camera roll has no row for any of it.

It can't be handed over

A camera roll is yours. It has your screenshots, your receipts, your parking spot, your work whiteboards. There is no moment where you hand your child twenty years of that and say: this is yours now. The good parts are in there, mixed with everything else in your life, and separating them out is a project — one you'd be doing in a single sitting, years later, from memory. An archive you can hand over has to be kept separately, on purpose, from the start.

And if you read that and think the camera roll is enough, it might be. That's a real answer.
