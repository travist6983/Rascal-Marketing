The same questions to ask your 4 year old still work at nine

Age bands are a starting filter, not a rule. “What are they pretending to be this week?” is as good at nine as at four — the answer just grows up with them, which is the point of asking it more than once.

How to use these without the app

Genuinely: take them. Put one in your calendar for 7am. Answer it in your notes app, or on paper, or in the back of whatever notebook is nearest. Two sentences is plenty, and the entry you actually write beats the essay you meant to write.

Three things make an answer survive twenty years, and none of them need software: the date, their age, and the question you were answering. Write those down with it. That third one is the one everybody skips, and it's the one that turns a note into something findable.

Why they're written like this

Every prompt asks for one concrete, observable thing. Not “capture a special moment” — an assignment like that is why keepsake books die on page seven. “What word do they mispronounce that you don't want to correct?” is answerable in nine seconds because there is exactly one right answer and you already know it.

They're also written to be read by any family. The bodies store pronoun tokens and the name as placeholders, and each one is filled in the moment before you read it — so the app says her to a family with a daughter and them to a family that hasn't said. On this page there's no child to ask, so they render neutrally.
